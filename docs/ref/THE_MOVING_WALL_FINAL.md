---

We lived in a valley that had stopped surprising us.

The well was where the well had always been. Justice was a word our grandfathers handed down. Nobody opened the dictionary.

Then the wall of the meeting hall began to travel.

Not in a night. A thumb's width after one season. Another after the next. No crack. No thunder. The benches still faced the front. The flag still hung in the same corner. The word safety, spoken by the same mouths, now meant something you had to be watched to keep.

A boy marked the base of the wall in charcoal. His uncle laughed — you've grown, that's all. A year later the uncle was kind, and tired, and asked why the boy loved upsetting people who were only trying to keep the village safe.

Strangers came with lanterns. Same flame. Different cut of the wick. One night a man was a hero for asking who ate the grain. The next night the same man was a threat to the children for asking. Both nights the village was sure. Both nights sure about a different loaf of the same bread.

Friends stopped eating together. Nobody mentioned they were still chewing it.

A second wagon arrived with the books already balanced. You keep living — we'll handle the count. The sick were fed. The arguments went quiet. The quiet felt like safety.

A woman counted bowls the way she counted spoons. The ledger said the hall fed a thousand. She could not find a thousand mouths. She said it once, softly. They told her the world was dangerous, and dangerous meant stop counting. She said it twice. Her name went into a smaller book that is never opened in front of anyone. Her friends developed a talent for being busy.

Under the hall there was a cellar. Other books. Dates. Rooms. Faces turned from the lamp. Nobody waved those around. Everybody just knew. Loud men became reasonable overnight. In that season, reasonable meant sitting down.

An old woman watched white paint roll over the charcoal mark.

We used to sell you the dark and call it night, she said. Now we sell you the fear and call it dawn.

The woman with the bowls kept counting. Not because the wagons were leaving. The gap between the number and the mouths was the last measurement that still belonged to her.

One evening she understood the joke that is not funny. The hall never needed her to love the new wall. It only needed her too afraid to walk to it with a stick.

She walked anyway.

A few of us brought sticks. We did not agree on every hill. Some still flinched at the old words. Some kept the doors shut. We agreed on three things. The wall had moved. The cellar door was real. A story that arrives already wearing your fear was cut for you.

This is an indisputable fact.

The strangers smiled when they saw the sticks. They had the smile ready before we finished arriving.

Charcoal is free. The walk is short.

The wall moved.

What did you call the space it left?
