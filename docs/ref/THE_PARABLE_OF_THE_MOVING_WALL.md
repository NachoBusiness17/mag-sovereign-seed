**The Parable of the Moving Wall**

There was a village that sat in a valley so familiar the people stopped looking at it. The well was where the well had always been. The road bent the same way. Justice meant the thing it had meant when their grandfathers said the word.

Then the wall of the meeting hall began to travel.

Not in a night. Not with a crash. A thumb's width after harvest, another after the next winter. No one felt an earthquake. The benches still faced forward. The preacher still pointed at the same painted sun. Only the sun was no longer quite over the same hill, and the word Justice, said in the same mouth, now landed on a different chore.

A boy who liked to measure things with a stick marked the base of the wall in charcoal. When he showed the mark to his uncle, the uncle laughed and said the boy had grown, that was all. When he showed it again a year later, the uncle was kind, and tired, and asked if the boy enjoyed upsetting people who were only trying to keep the hall warm.

The strangers did not move the wall with their hands. They arrived with lanterns that flickered a different story every evening. Same flame, different cut of the wick. One night the farmer was a hero for sharing seed. The next night the same farmer was a hoarder for asking who had eaten it. Both nights the village was sure. Both nights the village was sure about different men. Friends stopped eating at the same table. They did not notice they were still chewing the same bread.

A second wagon came with books already balanced. "You keep living," they said. "We will keep the count." The sick were fed. The arguments about debt went quiet. It tasted like relief.

A woman who stacked bowls at supper began to count heads the way she counted spoons. The ledger said the hall fed a thousand. She could not find a thousand mouths. She said so once, softly. They told her the system was complex, and that complexity was a kind of kindness. She said so twice. Her name appeared in a smaller book that was never opened in public. After that her friends developed a new talent for being busy.

In the cellar under the hall there were other books. These had dates and rooms and faces turned away from the light. The strangers did not brandish them. They only let it be known that such pages existed. Loud men became reasonable overnight. Reason, in that season, meant sitting down.

An old woman who still remembered when the wall had stood still watched the boy's charcoal mark disappear under a fresh coat of white. "They used to sell you the dark and call it night," she said. "Now they sell you the drift and call it dawn."

The woman who counted bowls kept counting. Not because she thought the wagons would leave. Because the gap between the number and the mouth was the last measurement that still belonged to her.

One evening she understood the joke that was not funny: the hall did not require her to love the new sun. It only required her to be too weary to walk to the wall with a stick.

She walked anyway.

A few others brought sticks. They did not agree on every hill. They agreed the wall had moved, and that the cellar door was real, and that a story which arrives already wearing your anger is a story that was cut for you.

The strangers smiled when they saw the sticks.

The smile had to travel farther than the wall had.
