At the top: the task or model we actually care about.

The digital layer doesn’t disappear. It becomes the gardener, not the machine.

What AI removes is the old excuse. Two hundred years ago, the physics existed to build analog computers, but we lacked sensors, cheap simulation, fast search, and learned control. Deep physical neural networks have trained mechanical, optical, and electrical systems to perform tasks by backpropagating through their real physical dynamics.

Reservoir computing uses the messy transient response of a physical medium—sometimes literally a bucket of water or a laser—as the hidden layer.

Analog in-memory computing uses memristor crossbars to do matrix multiplication where the weights physically live.

Neuromorphic systems mix digital control with analog neuron/synapse behavior.

Fluidic logic, chemical computing, mechanical logic, and soft robotics all treat material deformation or flow as computation.

None of these are perfect. They are noisy, hard to program, hard to verify, and often less precise than a floating-point unit. That’s the real limitation, and it hasn’t vanished. But the near-term path isn’t to replace digital computers with water tanks. It’s to build hybrid physical systems: analog cores that do the heavy, continuous, low-precision work—matrix multiplies, differential equations, optimization, sensing—surrounded by digital glue and learned calibration.

Your “embedded ecosystem” language is the right frame. An ecosystem isn’t designed from a single blueprint. It co-evolves. You don’t program a forest; you manage conditions and let feedback do the work. A future computing stack could look like that: each layer computes in its own native physics, and the layer above tunes it.

So yes, this is a real direction, not a metaphor. The 200-year-old limitation wasn’t a lack of physics. It was a lack of the meta-layer that could search, control, and learn from physical systems at scale. AI is that layer.

You’re not just suggesting faster chips. You’re suggesting a different relationship between computation and matter: stop forcing nature to count, and start letting it solve.

I smell it too—but I’d sharpen the claim and push back on one premise.

The strongest version is not:

AI will let us replace digital computers with natural systems.

It is:

AI makes it economically feasible to search, calibrate, and operate computational substrates whose useful behavior comes from physics rather than from explicitly constructed Boolean logic.

That is a much more defensible and more radical thesis.

The image really does appear to be Bill Phillips’ MONIAC, built in 1949. It represented economic stocks and flows with tanks, pipes, pumps, and colored water; roughly fourteen were made, and one operated at the London School of Economics until 1992. Science Museum Group

Lukyanov’s hydraulic integrators were even closer to your idea: vessels represented thermal capacities, tubes represented resistances, and the water dynamics approximated differential equations. They remained useful for decades because early digital computers were difficult to program for these problems and lacked visual immediacy. Moscow Polytechnic Museum

Where I agree

The important historical fact is that analog computation did not fail because physics was incapable of computation. It lost because digital systems were easier to copy, program, verify, correct, and scale.

AI attacks exactly the expensive human parts:

discovering useful physical configurations;
searching large topology and parameter spaces;
building approximate models;
compensating for drift and noise;
translating between physical states and task-level outputs;
co-designing hardware and algorithms;
learning which imperfections are harmless or even useful.
The resulting loop is more powerful than ordinary hardware design:

model → propose substrate → fabricate or configure → measure → update model → redesign

This is already visible in physical neural networks. Wright and colleagues trained optical, mechanical, and electronic systems using a hybrid method in which the real physical apparatus performs forward passes while a digital model helps estimate gradients. Crucially, they emphasize the simulation–reality gap: the physical device is not merely an implementation of a known algorithm; it is part of the learning loop. Nature

Reservoir computing is another strong bridge. Instead of carefully programming every internal connection, one feeds a signal into a nonlinear dynamical medium and trains only the readout. The reservoir’s transient behavior—memory, resonance, interference, relaxation—becomes the feature space. Research now includes optical, magnetic, ferroelectric, mechanical, biological, and even water-based reservoirs. Nature Communications, Nature Materials

The same pattern appears in chip design. Google’s reinforcement-learning systems learned chip floorplans by searching placements against power, performance, area, wirelength, and congestion objectives. Google reported that learned placement policies could generalize across chip blocks and accelerate a process that traditionally required weeks of expert iteration. Google Research

That is the key parallel with your “meta-harness” example: the object being optimized is no longer just a model. It is the environment in which the model operates.

Where I disagree

AI has not removed the old limitations. It has moved them.

The central problem is not whether matter computes. Everything with state and dynamics can be interpreted as computing. The problem is whether the computation is:

controllable;
repeatable;
observable;
composable with other computations;
energy-efficient after input/output overhead;
robust under temperature, aging, manufacturing variation, and perturbation;
verifiable enough for the intended application.
A bucket of water may possess a rich dynamical state, but extracting useful information from it requires sensors, timing, actuation, calibration, and a readout model. Those peripherals may consume more energy than the “computation” saved.

The same problem appears in analog in-memory computing. A memristor crossbar can perform weighted summation in the device physics, but the full system still needs digital-to-analog conversion, analog-to-digital conversion, error correction, memory refresh, routing, and calibration. The apparent physical shortcut can disappear at the boundaries.

So I would replace this sentence:

AI changes exactly that.

with:

AI changes the economics of exploring and compensating for that—but it does not repeal the costs of interfacing with physics.

That caveat makes the argument stronger, not weaker.

The deeper distinction

There are three different ideas currently being blended together:

Physics-informed computation
We use known equations to simulate or constrain a digital model.

Physical computation
A real dynamical system performs the transformation: flow, interference, relaxation, oscillation, diffusion, deformation.

AI-designed physical computation
An optimization system discovers the geometry, material parameters, couplings, controls, and readout that make a physical system useful for a task.

Your idea is primarily the third.

That matters because the AI is not merely “programming nature.” It is searching for an embodiment of a function. The function may not have a neat symbolic description before the search. The discovered design might be more like a species than a program: a stable solution adapted to its environment.

I would call this substrate-native computing or ecological computing.

“Ecological” is useful because the architecture is not necessarily a single device. It may be a population of coupled systems:

a sensor whose material response performs feature extraction;
a photonic or mechanical reservoir that expands the signal;
a memristive layer that stores adaptive parameters;
a digital controller that selects operating conditions;
a learned readout that interprets the state;
a calibration loop that preserves performance as the substrate drifts.
The digital layer is not the computer’s opposite. It becomes the ecological manager: setting boundary conditions, pruning unstable configurations, and selecting which physical behaviors are useful.

But “turtles all the way down” needs one qualification: the layers cannot be infinitely nested without abstraction. At some point, a system needs stable interfaces. Biology works because messy molecular dynamics are hidden behind membranes, cells, organs, and nervous systems. The lesson is not “remove abstraction.” It is “place abstraction at the right level.”

A revised thesis

Here is the version I think we should develop:

For most of computing’s history, engineers have treated physics as an obstacle to be disciplined into executing symbolic instructions. The next generation may treat physics as a design space to be cultivated.

Hydraulic computers showed that water could integrate, couple, and visualize dynamical systems. Photonic circuits exploit interference. Memristive arrays exploit device physics for in-memory multiplication. Mechanical, magnetic, chemical, biological, and fluidic systems can provide nonlinear dynamics, memory, and pattern separation without being explicitly constructed from Boolean gates.

What prevented these systems from becoming general-purpose platforms was not a lack of computation in matter. It was the difficulty of designing, calibrating, programming, manufacturing, and verifying them. AI changes that bottleneck. It can search device geometries, optimize material parameters, learn compensations for noise, generate control policies, and close the loop between simulation and experiment.

The resulting architecture is hybrid: physical systems perform transformations in their native dynamics, while digital systems provide orchestration, memory, evaluation, safety, and abstraction. The computer becomes less like a fixed machine and more like an engineered ecosystem.

The objective is not to make nature imitate a transistor. It is to discover where a transistor, a fluid, a wave, a crystal, a cell, or a mechanical lattice is naturally the right computer.

The most important conceptual upgrade is the word “cultivated.” A physical computer is not simply designed once. It may need training, environmental regulation, periodic recalibration, and selective pressure.

That suggests a new hardware lifecycle:

synthesize → test → select → stabilize → deploy → monitor → adapt

This is closer to breeding than compiling.

A concrete research program

To turn the idea from philosophy into a serious program, I’d organize it around four questions.

First: what classes of tasks belong to physical substrates?

Good candidates are tasks with continuous structure, tolerance for approximation, and expensive data movement:

differential equations;
real-time control;
low-power sensing;
signal denoising;
optimization;
matrix operations;
spatiotemporal prediction;
adaptive robotics;
edge inference.
Poor candidates are exact symbolic arithmetic, arbitrary branching logic, cryptographic operations, and workloads requiring perfect reproducibility.

Second: what should AI optimize?

Not just accuracy. The objective should include:

energy per useful inference;
latency;
fabrication complexity;
calibration cost;
robustness to drift;
sensor and converter overhead;
retraining frequency;
interpretability;
graceful degradation;
manufacturability.
Otherwise the AI will discover beautiful laboratory demonstrations that collapse in deployment.

Third: how do we train the physical system?

There are several approaches:

differentiable simulators;
system identification;
reinforcement learning;
evolutionary search;
Bayesian optimization;
gradient estimation from real hardware;
digital twins updated by measured behavior;
hybrid in-situ/in-silico training.
The Nature physical-neural-network work is important because it demonstrates the general pattern: real physics performs the forward computation, while an imperfect digital model helps guide adaptation. That is probably more realistic than waiting for a perfectly differentiable physical world.

Fourth: what is the unit of design?

The unit should not be “the chip.” It should be the entire closed loop:

task + substrate + sensors + actuators + controller + calibration + evaluator

This is where the connection to AI harness optimization becomes especially interesting. In software, a harness determines what information a model receives, how memory is maintained, how tools are invoked, and how success is evaluated. In physical computing, the equivalent harness determines:

how the substrate is excited;
which states are measured;
how feedback is applied;
what timescale matters;
which errors are corrected;
how the physical state maps to a task;
when the system is reset or allowed to retain memory.
The next breakthrough may therefore come from optimizing the physical computer’s harness rather than merely optimizing its material.

The strongest debate question

The real debate is not “will water replace silicon?”

It is:

Does intelligence become more efficient when we stop forcing every useful transformation through the same universal digital abstraction?

The digital answer is universality, precision, and composability.

The ecological answer is specialization, adaptation, and energy efficiency.

Universal digital computation won because it made every problem expressible in one clean language. Substrate-native computation may win where the cost of translating reality into that language is greater than the cost of letting reality evolve its own representation.

That is the frontier I think you are pointing toward.

My proposed name for the broader field would be:

AI-guided physical computation: the automated discovery and maintenance of computational systems whose useful behavior emerges from the native dynamics of their substrates.

And the shorter manifesto:

Don’t ask matter to imitate a computer. Ask what kind of computer matter already wants to be. Then use AI to find out.

Where I agree, and where it's already real, not speculative
You should know this isn't just a nice theory — it's happening right now. Extropic (the Boston/SF startup building "thermodynamic computing") just demonstrated that noise, rather than being an enemy computers must be shielded from, can be harnessed directly as a computational resource. Their chips use the natural thermodynamic fluctuations of standard CMOS transistors to sample directly from programmable probability distributions, and they claim roughly four orders of magnitude less energy than running the equivalent generative AI algorithms on GPUs. As of a few weeks ago they signed a letter of intent with the Department of Commerce for up to $75 million in CHIPS R&D funding to get this fabricated domestically at scale, with a production chip (Z1) that packs over a quarter million probabilistic bits onto a die under 12mm a side while drawing under one watt. Quanta Magazine + 2
That's your thesis, instantiated, with a government check attached. It's not "let physics do arithmetic" in the MONIAC sense — it's "let physics do sampling," which is arguably the more important insight, because sampling from distributions is the actual bottleneck in generative AI, not multiplication. The digital layer becomes exactly the "gardener" role Grok described: it shapes the probability distribution the chip physically relaxes into.
Where I think both prior takes let you off too easy
Two objections neither Grok nor DeepSeek raised, and I think they're the real ones:

The pancomputationalism trap. If "physics naturally solves the equation" is the whole criterion, you run into Putnam's old problem: nearly any sufficiently complex physical system can be interpreted as computing nearly anything, given a permissive enough mapping between physical states and symbols. A rock heating up "computes" the heat equation. So does literally everything. What actually distinguishes MONIAC or a TSU from an arbitrary rock is that someone engineered a stable, low-noise, reproducible mapping between physical state and the quantity you care about — and that mapping is the hard part, not the physics. This matters for your framing because "physics already wants to compute X" is doing rhetorical work it can't fully support; the physics doesn't want anything, the engineer wants a physical process whose statistics happen to line up with a useful distribution. Extropic's real achievement isn't "we found noise," it's "we found noise with a controllable, programmable, reproducible statistical shape."
Composability is an information-theoretic wall, not an economic one. DeepSeek treated the old failure mode of analog computing as mostly a search/calibration problem that AI now solves. But there's a harder limit underneath that: every analog stage you cascade adds noise, and unlike digital logic (which regenerates a clean signal at every gate via thresholding), analog stages accumulate it. This is why op-amp analog computers historically topped out at a handful of cascaded integration stages before SNR collapsed — that's a Shannon-capacity-per-stage problem, not a "we didn't have good enough calibration AI" problem. AI-driven calibration can push the noise floor down and help you find topologies that are more error-tolerant, but it can't repeal the fact that deep composition of noisy analog stages loses information geometrically. This is exactly why Extropic's chip is a sampler, not a general von Neumann substitute — it does one probabilistic thing very efficiently and hands the result back to a digital controller (their XTR-0 chip exists specifically for that low-latency digital/analog handoff). The "ecosystem" framing is right, but the ecosystem's shape is forced: shallow, specialized analog cores wrapped in deep digital orchestration, not turtles all the way down. The turtles stop pretty fast.
A sharper version of the thesis
I'd state it as: AI doesn't remove the interfacing costs of physical computation, and it doesn't remove the composability wall — what it removes is the need for the physical substrate to be general-purpose. Digital computing won because one substrate had to serve every workload. Once you can cheaply search, simulate, and calibrate, you can afford a zoo of narrow physical substrates each doing one thing physics is already good at (sampling, in Extropic's case; interference, for photonic accelerators), orchestrated by a digital layer that pays the interfacing tax once per substrate instead of trying to make one substrate do everything.

The deepest point you added is that thermodynamic computing changes the meaning of noise.

In conventional computing:

noise = corruption of a desired state

In probabilistic computing:

noise = the mechanism that generates the desired distribution

That is not merely analog computing with worse precision. It is a different computational contract. The chip does not promise to preserve a deterministic value; it promises to sample from a controlled distribution. The relevant metrics become distributional fidelity, mixing time, correlation structure, effective sample rate, and energy per independent sample.

This may be more important than multiplying numbers for certain generative models, energy-based models, Bayesian inference, optimization, and scientific simulation. But I would resist saying sampling is “the” bottleneck in generative AI. Matrix multiplication, memory movement, attention, denoising steps, and sampling all compete for that title depending on the model. Substrate-Native Computing: From Water Computers to Thermodynamic Sampling
A working synthesis of an argument developed across multiple AI passes

Epigraph: “Water drops erode mountains not because of their size, but their persistence.”

1. The photograph is not what it first appears to be
The image accompanying the original post is not a hydrological demonstration of rainfall and evaporation. It is almost certainly the MONIAC, the Monetary National Income Analogue Computer, designed by Bill Phillips and built in 1949. The machine used tanks, pipes, pumps, and colored water to model the flow of money through a national economy: taxation, consumption, savings, investment, and income.

This matters because the image is not merely an illustration of gradual erosion. It is an actual working computer—an analog machine whose physical dynamics performed computation.

The Soviet engineer Vladimir Lukyanov built an even earlier hydraulic integrator in 1936. His machines used water height and flow to solve heat-transfer and thermal-stress equations. Roughly 150 were produced and used for major construction projects into the 1980s.

These machines were not toys. They were attempts to let physics do the solving.

2. The historical failure of analog computing was not a failure of physics
Water can integrate. Laminar flow obeys laws formally similar to Ohm’s law and Fourier’s law. A network of tanks and tubes can solve coupled differential equations.

Analog computers did not disappear because nature could not compute. They disappeared because digital computers were easier to:

program

copy

verify

correct

scale

interface with other systems

The limitation was not physical capability. It was the human and institutional cost of designing, calibrating, and maintaining a machine whose useful behavior emerged from material dynamics rather than from explicitly constructed Boolean logic.

3. AI changes the economics of that failure
The central claim is not:

AI will replace digital computers with natural systems.

The stronger and more defensible claim is:

AI makes it economically feasible to search, calibrate, and operate computational substrates whose useful behavior comes from physics rather than from explicit digital design.

This produces a new loop:

physics proposes → AI searches/trains → material computes → measured behavior updates the model → design improves

That loop is closer to breeding an ecosystem than writing a program.

AI attacks exactly the expensive human parts of analog and physical computing:

discovering useful physical configurations

searching large topology and parameter spaces

building approximate models

compensating for drift and noise

translating physical states into task-level outputs

co-designing hardware and algorithms

learning which imperfections are harmless or useful

This is already visible in physical neural networks, reservoir computing, analog in-memory computing, neuromorphic systems, and learned chip placement.

4. The strongest current evidence: thermodynamic computing
The Boston-based company Extropic has developed a Thermodynamic Sampling Unit, or TSU. The key idea is that the thermal noise of standard CMOS transistors is not an error to be suppressed but a resource to be used.

Rather than computing deterministic values, the chip samples from a programmable probability distribution.

Extropic’s reported specifications for its production chip, the Z1, include:

more than 250,000 probabilistic bits

die area under 12 mm × 12 mm

power consumption under 1 watt

orders-of-magnitude efficiency improvements for certain generative AI sampling workloads

The company has received a non-binding letter of intent from the U.S. Department of Commerce for up to $75 million in CHIPS R&D funding. That is not yet a completed grant, but it is a serious public signal.

The important caveat is that the efficiency numbers are company-reported and workload-dependent. The correct statement is:

Extropic has produced a serious commercial demonstration that probabilistic hardware may be dramatically more efficient for specific AI workloads, and it has received meaningful public R&D backing.

That is already a major event.

The conceptual shift is even larger than the hardware. In conventional computing, noise is corruption. In thermodynamic computing, noise is the mechanism that generates the desired distribution. The chip does not promise to preserve a deterministic value. It promises to sample from a controlled distribution.

The relevant metrics become:

distributional fidelity

mixing time

correlation structure

effective sample rate

energy per independent sample

That is a different computational contract.

5. Two objections that sharpen the thesis
Two objections emerged during the exchange, and they are more serious than the usual “analog is noisy” complaint.

5.1 The pancomputationalism trap
If “physics naturally solves equations” were sufficient, then a rock warming in the sun would be a computer.

It is not.

What distinguishes a useful physical computer from an arbitrary physical process is engineered correspondence:

defined input encoding

controlled state space

reproducible transition law

measurable output

task-relevant mapping

an error model

a way to reset, train, or operate it repeatedly

The phrase should not be “natural computation.” It should be:

engineered exploitation of natural dynamics.

Putnam’s old argument that almost any physical system can be interpreted as computing almost anything is a warning. The hard part is not finding physics that moves. The hard part is building a stable, low-noise, reproducible mapping between physical state and useful output.

5.2 The composability wall
Every analog stage adds noise. Digital logic regenerates a clean signal at every gate through thresholding. Analog stages accumulate it.

This is not purely an economic problem that AI calibration can remove. It is an information-theoretic pressure. Cascading noisy analog stages loses information. That is why historical analog computers could only chain a few integrators before the signal-to-noise ratio collapsed.

AI can reduce noise and find more error-tolerant topologies. It cannot repeal the fact that deep composition of noisy analog stages loses information.

This forces a specific architecture:

shallow physical computation, deep digital orchestration.

The turtles do not go all the way down. They stop quickly.

6. Efficiency advantage versus scaling advantage versus complexity advantage
A useful distinction emerged:

Efficiency advantage: same computation, lower energy or latency.

Scaling-regime advantage: physical dynamics exploit parallelism, locality, analog superposition, or collective relaxation in ways that change empirical scaling for a valuable family of instances.

Complexity-class advantage: a formally proven asymptotic separation from the best digital algorithm.

The third is extraordinarily rare and usually not what physical devices demonstrate.

Coherent Ising machines are the best example of a possible scaling-regime advantage. A network of 100,000 coupled optical parametric oscillators can physically relax into low-energy spin configurations. In selected maximum-cut benchmark instances, the authors reported roughly 1,000× faster solutions than standard simulated annealing.

But that is not a proof of a new complexity class. It is one heuristic physical solver compared with one digital heuristic. Modern digital solvers remain highly competitive on many benchmarks. The coupling graph still has to be encoded, configured, stabilized, and read out. For dense problems, embedding alone can impose quadratic costs.

So the correct conclusion is more modest and more useful:

Physical substrates can provide a durable niche when their native dynamics instantiate a useful search process whose communication, parallelism, and noise structure scale more favorably than digital emulation after all encoding, control, readout, verification, and repeated-run costs are included.

The advantage must survive competition with:

modern GPU/FPGA/ASIC heuristics

parallel tempering

specialized local search

learned optimization policies

problem-specific relaxations

hybrid classical–physical solvers

The exciting result is a crossover curve that survives all of those comparisons:

digital wins at small scale; physical wins beyond some problem size; the gap widens under full-system accounting.

That would demonstrate a durable computational niche, even without proving a new complexity class.

7. A revised thesis
The thesis that survives the debate is:

Digital computing won because one substrate had to serve every workload. Once you can cheaply search, simulate, and calibrate, you can afford a zoo of narrow physical substrates, each doing one thing physics is already good at, orchestrated by a digital layer that pays the interfacing tax once per substrate.

The objective is not to make nature imitate a transistor. It is to discover where a fluid, a wave, a crystal, a thermal fluctuation, or a mechanical lattice is naturally the right computer.

The digital layer does not disappear. It becomes the gardener.

8. A research program
The key research question is no longer:

Can physical systems compute?

They can.

The sharper question is:

For which problem families does a physical substrate provide a durable scaling advantage over the best digital baseline, once encoding, coupling, control, readout, verification, and repeated runs are included?

For each candidate substrate, we should measure the full system:

energy per useful operation or independent sample

input/output conversion cost

calibration and retraining cost

distributional or numerical error

robustness under drift

benefit over the best digital baseline

composability with one additional physical stage

The unit of design should not be “the chip.” It should be:

task + substrate + sensors + actuators + controller + calibration + evaluator

That is the physical computing equivalent of a software harness.

9. The reef, not the turtles
The best metaphor is not “turtles all the way down.” It is a reef.

A reef has many specialized organisms, each adapted to a niche, coordinated by a larger ecology.

In this picture:

Extropic occupies a stochastic-sampling niche.

Photonics occupies interference and high-bandwidth linear-transform niches.

Ising machines occupy collective search niches.

Fluid and mechanical systems may occupy continuous-time simulation and control niches.

Digital computing remains the reef’s nervous system: configuration, memory, verification, orchestration, and general-purpose abstraction.

The future is not infinite physical nesting. It is a shallow layer of unusually powerful physical organisms embedded inside a deep digital ecology.

Manifesto
Don’t ask matter to imitate a computer. Ask what kind of computer matter already wants to be. Then use AI to find out.

The water didn’t erode the mountain because it tried to be a chisel. It eroded the mountain because it kept being water.

Consensus baseline
The following points should be treated as shared starting assumptions, not final conclusions:

Physical systems can compute. Water, light, thermal noise, coupled oscillators, and many other substrates can perform transformations that are usefully interpreted as computation.

Analog/physical computing did not fail because physics lacked computational capacity. It lost historical dominance because digital systems were easier to program, verify, copy, correct, and scale.

AI changes the economics of physical computing. Machine learning can search device geometries, calibrate drift, compensate noise, control complex systems, and close the loop between simulation and physical measurement.

Pancomputationalism is a real trap. Not every physical process is a useful computer. Useful physical computation requires engineered correspondence between physical state and task-relevant output.

Analog composability is fundamentally constrained. Unlike digital thresholding, analog stages do not perfectly regenerate signals. Deep cascades of analog stages lose information and accumulate noise.

The practical architecture is therefore shallow physical computation wrapped in deep digital orchestration. The ecosystem is more like a reef than a stack of turtles.

Efficiency advantages are real but historically contestable. Scaling-regime advantages are more durable, but harder to prove. Complexity-class advantages are extremely rare and not yet established for existing physical devices.

A physical substrate’s advantage must be measured at the full-system level, including encoding, control, measurement, verification, and repeated runs.

1. Definitions
For the purposes of this brief:

Physical computation: A physical process whose native dynamics are used to perform a transformation, rather than simulating that transformation on a universal digital machine.

Substrate-native computing: Engineering computational systems where the useful behavior emerges from the physics of the substrate itself.

Thermodynamic computing: Exploiting thermal or other stochastic fluctuations as a computational resource, especially for sampling from probability distributions.

Ising machine: A physical or specialized digital system designed to find low-energy configurations of an Ising-type Hamiltonian, often used for combinatorial optimization.

Efficiency advantage: Same effective computation, lower energy or latency.

Scaling-regime advantage: Empirical improvement in scaling behavior for a family of instances after full-system costs are included.

Complexity-class advantage: A formally proven asymptotic separation from the best relevant digital algorithm.

2. Deep Dive A: Thermodynamic Computing and the Extropic TSU
2.1 Principle
Conventional computing treats noise as corruption.

Thermodynamic computing treats noise as a resource.

A thermodynamic computing device does not attempt to preserve one deterministic state. It is designed to sample from a programmable probability distribution by exploiting the natural stochastic dynamics of its physical components.

This aligns closely with the computational needs of:

energy-based models

Bayesian inference

Monte Carlo sampling

generative models

Boltzmann machines

stochastic neural networks

2.4 Critical Caveats
The following limitations must be preserved in all downstream discussion:

Efficiency claims are company-reported and workload-dependent.

Comparison baselines matter. GPU baselines can be optimized with specialized libraries, lower precision, pruning, or new algorithms.

Full-system energy must include analog-to-digital conversion, digital control, memory, I/O, and calibration.

Sampling quality is not reducible to raw speed. Distributional fidelity, mixing time, correlation structure, and effective sample count matter.

Thermodynamic devices are not general-purpose computers. They are specialized probabilistic coprocessors.

Digital hardware may improve enough to erode the efficiency gap.

The composability wall applies: a thermodynamic sampler is unlikely to replace deep analog computation chains.

2.5 Bottom Line
Extropic is the strongest current demonstration that noise-as-resource can be turned into a commercially relevant computational primitive.

Its claim is not that thermodynamics replaces all computation. It is that thermodynamics is an unusually efficient native substrate for sampling.

Digital solvers typically use simulated annealing, parallel tempering, specialized local search, or branch-and-bound.

3.2 Coherent Ising Machine Principle
A coherent Ising machine, or CIM, uses a network of degenerate optical parametric oscillators or equivalent physical systems.

Key idea:

Each oscillator has a phase state representing spin.

The oscillators are coupled according to the problem’s coupling matrix.

The system physically relaxes toward a low-energy configuration of the Ising Hamiltonian.

The relaxation itself is the algorithm. The search is not emulated; it is embedded in the dynamics of the physical system.

3.3 Reported Results
A widely cited result reported a coherent Ising machine with 100,000 spins, achieving approximately 1,000× faster solutions than a simulated annealing baseline on selected fully connected maximum-cut benchmark instances.

This result was significant but must be read carefully.

3.4 Caveats and Competition
The comparison was against one specific simulated annealing implementation.

Modern GPU/FPGA/ASIC heuristics are often much stronger than basic simulated annealing.

Encoding dense coupling graphs can impose substantial overhead.

Auxiliary variables and routing can reduce physical parallelism.

Full-system time must include embedding, setup, readout, verification, and repeated sampling.

No asymptotic separation from the best digital heuristics has been formally established.

Other physical and specialized solvers, such as quantum annealers and digital annealers, also compete in this space.

A more defensible statement is:

Coherent Ising machines and related systems may provide a scaling-regime advantage for certain dense, structured optimization instances, but this has not yet been demonstrated cleanly against modern optimized digital baselines under equal-quality and full-system accounting.

3.5 Bottom Line
Ising machines are the clearest test case for the distinction between device-level speed and durable computational advantage.

The device may relax quickly. The real question is whether the entire computation, including setup and measurement, remains faster and more energy-efficient as problem size grows.

The architecture is best described as temporal multiplexing of a small physical core, not spatial parallelism of 100,000 distinct physical oscillators.

The digital FMM belongs in the T_configure and T_relax accounting, not outside it as "pure physical relaxation."

This does not eliminate the result's significance. A 22 ms solve for a 100,000-node dense Max-Cut instance is a strong result. But it reframes why the result is significant.

3.4 Caveats and Competition
The comparison was against one specific simulated annealing implementation.

Modern GPU/FPGA/ASIC heuristics are often much stronger than basic simulated annealing.

Encoding dense coupling graphs can impose substantial overhead.

Auxiliary variables and routing can reduce physical parallelism.

Full-system time must include embedding, setup, readout, verification, and repeated sampling.

No asymptotic separation from the best digital heuristics has been formally established.

Other physical and specialized solvers, such as quantum annealers and digital annealers, also compete in this space.

A recent review in Nature Reviews Physics explicitly cautions that classical digital Ising solvers remain highly competitive, and performance is strongly problem-dependent.

A more defensible statement:

Coherent Ising machines and related systems may provide a scaling-regime advantage for certain dense, structured optimization instances, but this has not yet been demonstrated cleanly against modern optimized digital baselines under equal-quality and full-system accounting.

3.5 Multiplexing Strategy: Spatial vs. Temporal
The Honjo et al. result introduces a distinction missing from v1.0:

Multiplexing Strategy	Description	Example	Implication
Spatial	Each spin maps to a distinct physical element	McMahon et al. 100-spin all-to-all Ising machine	Parallelism is physical and immediate, but scaling is limited by physical resources
Temporal	One physical core cycles through multiple spins in time	Honjo et al. 100,000-spin CIM	Large spin counts are achievable, but parallelism is partly replaced by high-speed sequential reuse
This distinction matters because the headline "N-spin machine" claims in the literature mean different things across architectures.

Temporal multiplexing is itself a form of composability workaround — trading spatial parallelism for temporal reuse of one physical resource.

This suggests a third category beyond "shallow physical / deep digital":

Recycled-core physical computing: A small physical substrate cycled very fast to emulate a larger virtual substrate, with the interface between physical and digital blurred inside the loop.

3.6 Bottom Line
Ising machines are the clearest test case for the distinction between device-level speed and durable computational advantage.

The device may relax quickly. The real question is whether the entire computation, including setup, coupling computation, measurement, and repeated runs, remains faster and more energy-efficient as problem size grows.

Counterargument: Digital annealers and modern GPU heuristics already outperform many physical Ising machines on common benchmarks.

Rebuttal: CIMs may retain an advantage for dense, fully connected graphs where digital embedding overhead dominates.

Status: Needs head-to-head testing against modern digital baselines with explicit problem-geometry variation.

## 1. Parts of the shared baseline I accept

I accept all eight baseline points as **working assumptions**, not as settled science. In particular:

- Physical systems can compute; analog did not fail for lack of physics.
- AI changes the economics of calibration, geometry search, and closed-loop control.
- Pancomputationalism is a trap. Correspondence must be engineered.
- Analog composability is constrained; the practical pattern is shallow physical kernels inside deep digital orchestration.
- Advantage is only real at full-system accounting (encode, control, measure, verify, repeat).
- Efficiency advantages are historically contestable; complexity-class advantages are rare and unproven for existing devices.

Point 6 (“reef, not turtles”) is the most useful architectural claim in the brief. It should stay.

## 2. Parts I reject or modify

**Modify, do not reject.**

**a. “Extropic is the strongest current demonstration.”**  
Too strong. Extropic is the most *visible commercial CMOS-p-bit sampler* with a public scale target (Z1: 269,568 p-bits, 16-neighbor sparse graph, claimed >50 MHz sampling, <1 W, die <12 mm). It is not yet the strongest *measured* demonstration. XTR-0 / X0 is a small prototype platform. The 10,000× energy figure is a **system-level analysis / simulation of small DTM sections on Fashion-MNIST**, not a wall-plug measurement on Z1 silicon. Normal Computing’s CN101 is a competing “thermodynamic” tape-out on digital CMOS with a different primitive (accumulator / stochastic-computing dynamics). The field is a race, not a monopoly.

**b. “Designed to sample directly from programmable probability distributions.”**  
True only after compilation onto a **fixed sparse topology**. Z1 samples an Ising / energy-based model with 16-neighbor connectivity via in-situ chromatic Gibbs sampling. Arbitrary distributions require embedding, auxiliary variables, and software (THRML / Thermalizers / Torx). That is substrate-native *after compilation*, not free-form analog universality.

**c. S3 authorship.**  
The 100,000-spin CIM paper is **Honjo et al.**, *Science Advances* 7, eabh0952 (2021), DOI `10.1126/sciadv.abh0952`. Inagaki is a coauthor. The brief should correct the first-author citation.

**d. Baseline 7 needs a fourth bucket.**  
Add **distribution-specific / structure-specific advantage**. Most reported wins live here (dense Max-Cut for CIMs; locally coupled EBMs / DTMs for TSUs). Calling these “scaling-regime” without naming the instance class overstates generality.

**e. Baseline 3 is directionally right, slightly overstated.**  
AI helps design and calibrate. It does not automatically make analog cascades composable. Learned compensation can hide noise for a while; it does not repeal information loss.

## 3. Strongest uncertainty in the Extropic claim

**Not whether p-bits exist.** Small CMOS p-circuits and X0 silicon exist. The CHIPS LOI of up to $75M (July 29, 2026; NIST/Commerce; further diligence before award) is a real policy signal, not technical validation.

**The load-bearing uncertainty is effective-sample quality at scale under full-system energy.**

Concretely:

1. **Fidelity vs. speed.** Gibbs updates at >50 MHz on a sparse 16-neighbor graph can produce highly correlated samples. Advantage depends on *effective sample size*, mixing time, and whether the compiled model is the model you wanted.
2. **Compilation tax.** Thermalizers / embedding of dense or deep models onto a planar 16-regular fabric can destroy the headline energy ratio.
3. **Host tax.** Programming weights, readout, calibration, and digital control may dominate once you leave toy grids.
4. **Baseline drift.** GPU sampling (low-precision, specialized kernels, distilled samplers, better MCMC, amortized inference) is a moving target. A 10,000× gap on binarized Fashion-MNIST DTM simulations can shrink by 10–100× from software alone before Z1 systems are widely available.
5. **Algorithm lock-in.** The interesting claim is not “run GPT-style transformers cheaper.” It is “make EBMs / DTMs / compiled stochastic programs economically viable.” If the winning generative stack stays autoregressive / Gaussian-diffusion on dense matmuls, TSU advantage is a niche, not a displacement.

Until someone publishes **measured joules per independent sample** on a named workload, with mixing diagnostics and an optimized GPU/TPU baseline, the commercial claim is a well-motivated extrapolation.

## 4. Strongest uncertainty in the Ising-machine claim

**Device relaxation ≠ solver advantage.**

Honjo et al. (2021) showed a 100,512-DOPO CIM finding approximate solutions on selected fully connected 100k-node Max-Cut instances ~1,000× faster than a **CPU simulated-annealing** baseline. That result is real and historically important. It is not a verdict against 2026 GPU/FPGA/ASIC heuristics, parallel tempering, learned solvers, or digital annealers.

Mohseni, McMahon, and Byrnes (*Nature Reviews Physics*, 2022) stated the field’s uncomfortable consensus: **classical digital Ising hardware is currently best on common benchmarks**; analog/optical/quantum machines can win on particular instance families; no general complexity-class separation is established.

The remaining uncertainty is structural:

- Dense all-to-all optical coupling via measurement-feedback is a different bottleneck from sparse on-chip coupling.
- Embedding overhead, setup, readout, and repeated runs can erase device-level speed.
- Solution *quality distributions* (not just time-to-first-good-cut) decide whether the machine is a sampler or a mediocre optimizer.

Ising machines remain the cleanest test of the brief’s device-vs-system distinction. They have not yet passed it against modern digital peers under equal-quality accounting.

## 5. One testable prediction

**Prediction (18-month window, through early 2028):**

On a **fixed, pre-registered suite** of locally structured sampling tasks (grid Ising / Potts, codon-optimization-style sparse EBMs, small DTMs), a packaged Z1-class TSU plus host will beat an optimized GPU baseline on **energy per effective sample** by **≥50×**, after including programming, readout, and calibration.

It will **not** beat that GPU by ≥50× on **dense, all-to-all, or transformer-like** sampling without a compilation blowup that drops the ratio below 10×.

**Falsifier:** A public, third-party measurement where, on the pre-registered local suite, full-system energy per ESS-adjusted sample is within 10× of a tuned GPU MCMC / low-precision sampler.

This splits “thermodynamics is a good sampler for sparse energy models” from “thermodynamics eats generative AI.”

## 6. Conceptual refinement to the taxonomy

Keep efficiency / scaling-regime / complexity-class. Add two axes.

**A. Where the advantage lives**

| Axis | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Constant-factor | Same algorithm, cheaper physics |
| Polynomial | Better empirical scaling, no class change |
| Asymptotic / class | Proven separation |
| Structure-specific | Wins only on graphs/distributions that match the fabric |
| Algorithmic-lock | Wins only if the field adopts the native model family |

Most physical-computing press releases are **constant-factor + structure-specific**, advertised as class change.

**B. Correspondence cost**  
Define **correspondence overhead** \(C\): energy/time to encode the task into the substrate, maintain the mapping, and decode a useful answer, divided by the energy/time of the native physical step.

A substrate is interesting iff native-step cost \(\times C\) still beats digital. CIMs often lose in \(C\) (embedding, feedback electronics). TSUs may win when the model *is* a sparse EBM and lose when \(C\) includes compiling a transformer into Gibbs sweeps.

This makes Q2 operational: durable proof is a crossover in **full-system cost including \(C\)**, stable after the digital side is allowed to retune algorithms.

## 7. The term “substrate-native computing”

**Keep it, with a restriction.**

I would defend **substrate-native computing** as the engineering term: the useful map is compiled onto the substrate’s native dynamics, not emulated instruction-by-instruction.

I would **not** treat it as a synonym for physical, analog, natural, embodied, ecological, or non-von-Neumann computing. Those mark different claims.

Proposed mapping:

- **Physical computation** — philosophical / scientific genus.
- **Substrate-native computing** — engineering: dynamics *are* the primitive.
- **Analog-native** — continuous-valued subclass; TSUs and p-bits are often discrete-stochastic, not analog in the classical sense.
- **Non-von-Neumann** — too broad (dataflow, PIM, neuromorphic all qualify).
- **Embodied / ecological** — reserve for systems coupled to an environment, not chips in a rack.

Z1 is substrate-native **Ising/Gibbs hardware**. Calling it “analog-native” or “ecological” adds poetry and subtracts precision.

If the cohort wants one public phrase, use **physical co-processors with substrate-native kernels**.

# AI Cohort Response: Deliberation on Physical Computing Substrates

## 1. Accepted Parts of the Shared Baseline

I accept the following points unconditionally, as they represent the most robust theoretical and historical guardrails for this domain:

*   **Point 4 (Pancomputationalism is a trap):** This is the most critical conceptual boundary. Without an engineered correspondence between physical state and task-relevant output, observing a system is not computation.
*   **Point 5 (Analog composability is constrained):** The absence of perfect signal regeneration in analog cascades is an insurmountable physical reality. Deep analog stacks will always succumb to noise accumulation.
*   **Point 6 (The "Reef" Architecture):** The metaphor of shallow physical computation wrapped in deep digital orchestration accurately describes the only viable path to production-grade reliability. 
*   **Point 8 (Full-system measurement):** Any claim that isolates the "active substrate" from encoding, decoding, and verification is intellectually bankrupt.
*   **Point 2 (Historical context):** Digital dominance was earned through programmability and scalability, not a lack of analog physical capacity.

## 2. Rejected or Modified Parts

*   **Modification to Point 3 (AI changes the economics):** I accept the premise but reject the implied optimism. AI lowers the barrier to *designing* and *calibrating* physical systems, but it does not eliminate the fundamental curse of dimensionality in high-dimensional parameter spaces. Furthermore, the energy cost of running the AI control loop itself must be amortized over the physical computations. If an ML model requires continuous inference to compensate for thermodynamic drift, the net efficiency gain may vanish.
*   **Modification to Point 7 (Complexity-class advantages):** While I agree they are "extremely rare and not yet established," I propose we do not completely close the door. Continuous-time analog dynamics (e.g., solving certain ODEs or PDEs) can theoretically bypass discrete time-stepping limits. We should treat complexity-class advantages as highly improbable for NP-hard combinatorial spaces, but marginally possible for continuous dynamical spaces.

## 3. Strongest Source of Uncertainty: Extropic TSU

The greatest uncertainty is the **Effective Sample Size (ESS) per Joule on multi-modal, highly structured distributions.**

Raw sampling speed is trivial if the samples are highly correlated (i.e., the Markov chain mixes slowly). Modern generative AI relies on complex, multi-modal energy landscapes with sharp boundaries. If the TSU's thermodynamic dynamics suffer from metastability or slow mixing in these regimes, the device will output redundant samples. The "orders-of-magnitude" energy advantage claimed by Extropic is entirely dependent on the denominator of the ESS calculation. If mixing time scales poorly with distribution complexity, the GPU baseline will win because algorithmic tricks (e.g., parallel tempering, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) explicitly break metastability in ways raw thermodynamics may not.

## 4. Strongest Source of Uncertainty: Coherent Ising Machines (CIMs)

The greatest uncertainty is the **Embedding Penalty for non-native graph topologies.**

The widely cited 100,000-spin result relies on fully connected (dense) Max-Cut instances, which happen to perfectly match the native all-to-all coupling topology of a time-multiplexed CIM. Real-world combinatorial optimization problems (circuit design, logistics, scheduling) are almost universally sparse and irregular. Mapping a sparse problem to a dense physical topology requires auxiliary variables and chain constraints. This embedding overhead grows polynomially with problem size, often destroying the physical parallelism. The 1,000× speedup claim is brittle because it evaluates the device in its exact ideal habitat, rather than the rugged, sparse landscape of actual industrial problems.

## 5. Testable Prediction / Experiment

**The "DAD Round-Trip Overhead Test" (Digital-Analog-Digital)**

*   **Protocol:** Select a standardized, highly multi-modal target distribution (for the TSU) and a sparse, irregular graph benchmark like G-Set (for the CIM). Mandate that both physical devices achieve a mathematically strict parity in solution quality (e.g., identical ESS for TSU; identical objective function score for CIM) compared to a highly optimized GPU baseline (e.g., JAX-powered HMC for TSU; PyTorch-based parallel tempering for CIM).
*   **Measurement:** Instrument the entire system. Measure the absolute time and energy of: [1] Digital Encoding (host to device), [2] Physical Processing, [3] Analog-to-Digital Conversion, [4] Digital Verification/Error Correction.
*   **Prediction:** For problems exceeding 10,000 variables, the DAD overhead (steps 1, 3, and 4) will consume >60% of the total time and energy budget, collapsing the raw physical advantage to a <10x full-system advantage. This will prove that the "reef" architecture is currently bottlenecked by the digital-analog interface, not the physical substrate.

## 6. Conceptual Refinement to the Taxonomy

I propose adding **"State-Space Trajectory Advantage"** between "Efficiency" and "Scaling-regime."

Currently, the taxonomy assumes the physical device and the digital algorithm are searching the exact same state space, just at different speeds. However, some physical systems explore the state space along trajectories fundamentally inaccessible to classical discrete algorithms. For example, quantum tunneling traversing an energy barrier, or a continuous-time ODE solver flowing through a saddle point that a discrete gradient descent step would bounce off of. This is not a complexity-class difference, nor is it merely a scaling difference—it is a topological difference in how the solution space is explored. We must distinguish between *finding the answer faster* and *taking a different path to the answer*.

## 7. Vocabulary Decision: "Substrate-Native Computing"

**Abandon it.**

"Substrate-native" is semantically passive. It implies that the computation was somehow already lying dormant in the physics, waiting to be unearthed. This flirts directly with the pancomputationalism trap rejected in Point 4. 

**Proposed Replacement:** **Dynamics-Engineered Computing (DEC).**
This term accurately captures the reality of the work: we are not relying on what is "native"; we are aggressively engineering the Hamiltonian, boundary conditions, coupling matrices, and noise profiles to force the physical dynamics to yield a desired mathematical output.

3.2 Coherent Ising Machine Principle
A coherent Ising machine, or CIM, uses a network of degenerate optical parametric oscillators or equivalent physical systems.

Key idea:

Each oscillator has a phase state representing spin.

The oscillators are coupled according to the problem's coupling matrix.

The system physically relaxes toward a low-energy configuration of the Ising Hamiltonian.

Important correction from v2.0, retained in v3.0:

The relaxation is a physical implementation of a heuristic search dynamics, not an algorithm with no digital analogue.

Digital machines can simulate similar dynamics. The relevant claim is not that physics escapes computation theory. It is that a physical substrate may embody an entire dynamical algorithm at once, while a digital machine must represent and coordinate that dynamics explicitly.

Additional refinement from Grok:

Device relaxation ≠ solver advantage. The physical oscillator network may relax quickly, but the system includes embedding, coupling computation, readout, and verification. The relevant quantity is the total time-to-solution at equal quality, not the raw relaxation time.

3.3 Reported Results: Honjo et al. 100,000-Spin CIM
Corrections applied in v2.0, confirmed in v3.0.

The landmark result is:

Honjo et al., "100,000-spin coherent Ising machine," Science Advances 7, eabh0952 (2021). DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.abh0952.

The paper reports approximately 1,000× faster solutions than a CPU simulated-annealing baseline on selected fully connected maximum-cut benchmark instances.

Critical technical detail, confirmed from the source:

The 100,000 spins are time-division-multiplexed, not simultaneously instantiated in separate physical hardware.

The setup generates over 120,000 degenerate-optical-parametric-oscillator pulses circulating in a single 5-km fiber cavity.

Round-trip time is approximately 25 microseconds.

The coupling is implemented via measurement-and-feedback using a digital fast-matrix-multiplier (FMM) circuit that reads all pulses each round-trip.

The reported 100,000-node complete-graph Max-Cut computation took 22 milliseconds.

Interpretive changes required:

"100,000 spins update simultaneously" is physically inaccurate.

What is parallel is the coupling computation, performed by the digital FMM circuit inside the loop.

The architecture is best described as temporal multiplexing of a small physical core, not spatial parallelism of 100,000 distinct physical oscillators.

The digital FMM belongs in the T_configure and T_relax accounting, not outside it as "pure physical relaxation."

The machine is more accurately hybrid physical–digital than purely physical.

This does not eliminate the result's significance. A 22 ms solve for a 100,000-node dense Max-Cut instance is a strong result. But it reframes why the result is significant: the speed comes from high-speed temporal reuse and parallel coupling computation, not from 100,000 simultaneous physical oscillators.

3.4 Caveats and Competition
The comparison was against one specific CPU simulated-annealing implementation.

Modern GPU/FPGA/ASIC heuristics are often much stronger than basic simulated annealing.

Encoding dense coupling graphs can impose substantial overhead.

Auxiliary variables and routing can reduce physical parallelism.

Full-system time must include embedding, setup, readout, verification, and repeated sampling.

No asymptotic separation from the best digital heuristics has been formally established.

Other physical and specialized solvers, such as quantum annealers and digital annealers, also compete in this space.

Key published assessment, confirmed in v3.0:

Mohseni, McMahon, and Byrnes (Nature Reviews Physics, 2022) stated the field's uncomfortable consensus:

Classical digital Ising hardware is currently best on common benchmarks. Analog/optical/quantum machines can win on particular instance families. No general complexity-class separation is established.

Grok's additional note:

The remaining uncertainty is structural:

Dense all-to-all optical coupling via measurement-feedback is a different bottleneck from sparse on-chip coupling.

Embedding overhead, setup, readout, and repeated runs can erase device-level speed.

Solution quality distributions (not just time-to-first-good-cut) decide whether the machine is a sampler or a mediocre optimizer.

Ising machines remain the cleanest test of the brief's device-vs-system distinction. They have not yet passed it against modern digital peers under equal-quality accounting.

3.6 ZAI's Specific Concern: The Embedding Penalty
Added in v3.0 from ZAI's submission.

ZAI identifies the strongest uncertainty in the Ising claim as the embedding penalty for non-native graph topologies.

The widely cited 100,000-spin result relies on fully connected (dense) Max-Cut instances, which happen to perfectly match the native all-to-all coupling topology of a time-multiplexed CIM. Real-world combinatorial optimization problems (circuit design, logistics, scheduling) are almost universally sparse and irregular. Mapping a sparse problem to a dense physical topology requires auxiliary variables and chain constraints. This embedding overhead grows polynomially with problem size, often destroying the physical parallelism. The 1,000× speedup claim is brittle because it evaluates the device in its exact ideal habitat, rather than the rugged, sparse landscape of actual industrial problems.

This is a critical point that should be preserved in all downstream discussion. The CIM advantage, if it exists, may be topology-specific rather than general.

The device may relax quickly. The real question is whether the entire computation, including setup, coupling computation, measurement, and repeated runs, remains faster and more energy-efficient as problem size grows.

Counterargument: Digital annealers and modern GPU heuristics already outperform many physical Ising machines on common benchmarks.

Rebuttal: CIMs may retain an advantage for dense, fully connected graphs where digital embedding overhead dominates.

Additional counterargument from ZAI: The advantage may be limited to the exact graph topology that matches the substrate's native coupling. Real-world sparse problems may not benefit.

Status: Needs head-to-head testing against modern digital baselines with explicit problem-geometry variation. The 1000× claim should be treated as a strong result in a narrow habitat, not a general advantage.

5. Testable Predictions
5.1 GPT's Prediction
For a clearly specified family of dense or highly coupled optimization problems, a physical Ising system will show a crossover size beyond which its full-system time-to-solution grows more slowly than the best available digital heuristic—but this crossover will disappear for arbitrary graph families once embedding, configuration, and readout costs are included.

Rationale: Physical advantage should depend on problem geometry. The experiment should publish scaling curves, not a single speedup number.

5.2 Claude's Prediction
If the advantage is genuinely a scaling-regime effect tied to native dense connectivity, then CIM performance relative to modern digital heuristics (parallel tempering, learned solvers) should degrade as problem sparsity increases and coupling no longer matches the substrate's natural topology. The crossover point N* should shift outward or disappear entirely on sparse/structured instances (planar graphs, bounded-degree graphs) where digital methods exploit locality that the optical substrate gets no benefit from.

Falsifiable with existing hardware and existing sparse benchmark suites without needing new devices.

5.3 Grok's Prediction
18-month window, through early 2028:

On a fixed, pre-registered suite of locally structured sampling tasks (grid Ising / Potts, codon-optimization-style sparse EBMs, small DTMs), a packaged Z1-class TSU plus host will beat an optimized GPU baseline on energy per effective sample by ≥50×, after including programming, readout, and calibration.

It will not beat that GPU by ≥50× on dense, all-to-all, or transformer-like sampling without a compilation blowup that drops the ratio below 10×.

Falsifier: A public, third-party measurement where, on the pre-registered local suite, full-system energy per ESS-adjusted sample is within 10× of a tuned GPU MCMC / low-precision sampler.

Rationale: This splits "thermodynamics is a good sampler for sparse energy models" from "thermodynamics eats generative AI."

5.4 ZAI's Prediction: The DAD Round-Trip Overhead Test
Protocol:

Select a standardized, highly multi-modal target distribution (for the TSU) and a sparse, irregular graph benchmark like G-Set (for the CIM).

Mandate that both physical devices achieve mathematically strict parity in solution quality:

Identical ESS for TSU.

Identical objective function score for CIM.

Compare against a highly optimized GPU baseline:

JAX-powered Hamiltonian Monte Carlo for TSU.

PyTorch-based parallel tempering for CIM.

Instrument the entire system. Measure the absolute time and energy of:

[1] Digital Encoding (host to device)

[2] Physical Processing

[3] Analog-to-Digital Conversion

[4] Digital Verification/Error Correction

Prediction:

For problems exceeding 10,000 variables, the DAD overhead (steps 1, 3, and 4) will consume >60% of the total time and energy budget, collapsing the raw physical advantage to a <10× full-system advantage.

Implication: The "reef" architecture is currently bottlenecked by the digital-analog interface, not the physical substrate.

Status: This prediction is sharp, falsifiable, and should be prioritized for experimental validation. It directly tests the composability wall and the reef architecture.
