# THE FENCE — final draft with landmines embedded

The fence between Tom Hartley's place and the Dubois farm had been there since before either of them was born, which is how a thing gets real: nobody alive remembers voting on it. Gray boards, silvered by weather, the kind of fence you stop seeing. Tom grew tomatoes near the fence. Had for twelve years. The soil was good there. He'd won a ribbon at the county fair three times.

Then the county sent a letter.

The letter said the fence was wrong. Had always been wrong. A survey from 1987 placed the line three feet to the east. The new survey placed it four feet further. Tom's tomatoes were on Dubois land. His well was on Dubois land. The driveway he'd paved was on Dubois land.

Tom called the county. The clerk was polite. She said the 1987 survey was the legal record and the new survey corrected an error in the 1987 survey, which had corrected an error in an older survey, and the chain went back further than anyone could pull. She said the fence had never been in the right place. Tom had just been lucky. Somebody always owns the lucky.

Tom asked to see the 1987 survey. Archived. Tom asked to see the new survey. Proprietary — the firm owned the methodology.

Tom drove to the county seat and pulled the oldest map he could find. 1954. The fence was on it. Right where he'd built his tomatoes. He drove to the historical society. A map from 1921. Same fence. Same place.

He brought both maps to the county clerk. She said: those aren't the legal maps. The legal map is the current map. The current map is the one that matters.

Tom said: but the fence was there before the current map.

The clerk said: and now the current map is there before the fence.

She was not lying. She was reading.

He went home. Left the tomatoes. Wasn't stealing. A trespass is just a title that arrived late.

The letter came the next week. Trespass notice. White stakes in the ground where the old fence used to stand. Tom's garden was now labeled on a plat map: RESERVED — PENDING DEVELOPMENT.

He called Dubois. Old Man Dubois was quiet for a long time. Then he said: I know where it was, Tom. But I got the same letter.

The shadow you mail to your neighbor comes back addressed to you.

The next morning Tom took his charcoal and wrote on the biggest stake: I KNOW WHERE THE FENCE WAS.

A neighbor drove by and saw it. Then another. Then the woman from the farm bureau who'd given him the ribbon. Then people he didn't know. They didn't all agree on where the fence should be. But they all stopped. And they all looked at the stake. And they all remembered something they had stopped checking.

The fence was never wrong. It was early.
