## The footer line

The poster ends with an instruction, not a claim. "FOLLOW THE LIGHT WITHIN" sits beneath "Same Earth, Higher Realms, Many Perspectives, One Human Quest" on Matt's "RAINBOW ROAD: The Luminous Path Between Realms," and the claims audit declined to grade it: "Pure rhetoric/slogan; makes no factual assertion. Noted as such." [1] The auditor is right to pass over a slogan as inert. A lens exists to pick up what the auditor sets down. This lens picks up the footer and finds, folded inside it, the whole Eastern answer to the poster's question, arriving through the side door of Western devotional art.

The thesis under test: the East internalizes the bridge. Where the Norse road runs between external realms, laid from earth to heaven like a public work, the Eastern luminous path is built and become, not crossed. The road you stand on becomes the road you stir the world from; the road becomes the creature you read; the creature gives way to the mirror you are already inside; and at the far end the mirror becomes the body dissolving into light. Four stations along one gradient. At none of them does the Eastern register ask the traveler to walk.

The poster, read as a whole, never learns this. It traces a single register, the luminous path between realms, across eight waves, from Snorri's Bifröst through an aurora identification, a claimed 1942 Antarctic city, four maps, and Mario Kart's Rainbow Road, and its machinery is entirely external: theories, maps, cities, tracks. [2] Then, at the bottom edge, in small type, its own footer preaches the one move the poster's panels never make. The Eastern reading says this is not decoration. It says the register itself, pressed hard enough, turns inward, and that the poster's final line is the register preaching its own completion, likely without knowing it. Whether Matt knew is Unproven; the audit found no documented link between the poster's panels, and by the same standard no documented link can be shown between the poster and any Eastern source. What can be shown is the convergence, and convergence is the thing this lens is licensed to measure.

## I. The baseline: a bridge that stays outside you

Begin with what the audit nailed down, because the contrast only works if the Norse pole is exact. Snorri's Gylfaginning, c. 1220, gives Gangleri a bridge that is, in Brodeur's 1916 rendering the poster tracks near-verbatim, a thing "the gods made a bridge from earth to heaven, called Bifrost," of three colors, "very strong, and made with cunning and with more magic art than other works of craftsmanship." [3] The audit's verdict on the poster's compression: Supported, with one omission flagged. The omitted line is the one this essay will keep returning to: "That which thou seest to be red in the bow is burning fire," the fire Snorri sends to keep the frost-giants off the span. [3]

Everything in that chapter makes the bridge external. It is built, by someone, from one place to another place. It has a guard, Heimdall, posted at Himinbjörg. It carries traffic: the gods ride it daily to council at the well. It has a failure mode: it will break "when Muspell's sons ride it," as the audit summarizes the same chapter. [4] It even has a security doctrine, the burning red band, aimed at excluding a class of pedestrian. This is infrastructure, complete with maintenance concerns and an end-of-life schedule. The traveler crosses the bridge; the bridge remains a bridge; nobody becomes it. The redness is fire on the structure, not in the walker.

Notice also what the poster's Western lineage does to this object, per the digest's own inventory: it theorizes the bridge (the aurora identification; the strong attribution inside that wave is the audit's one Unproven), maps the bridge (1824 to 1982), builds the bridge (Hefferlin's Rainbow City, "multicoloured buildings, streets and roads," labeled LATER ACCOUNT, UNVERIFIED), and finally drives on the bridge (1992). [2] [5] Each wave makes the road more external, more object-like, more buildable. The Hefferlin manuscript's claim, as the audit quotes it, is that "Emery and some others... discovered Rainbow City in the fall of 1942," a city whose streets are plastic and whose buildings use every color of the bow. [5] The Western dream, at its far end, is to pave the rainbow. The Eastern texts move the other way. They begin with an arch and end with a self.

## II. The floating bridge: a standing place for creation

The Kojiki, compiled in 712 and translated by Basil Hall Chamberlain in 1882, opens with a bridge that behaves like no other in world literature. The Heavenly Deities commission the two who will become Izanagi and Izanami to "make, consolidate, and give birth to this drifting land," and hand them a heavenly jewelled spear. Chamberlain: "So the two Deities, standing upon the Floating Bridge of Heaven, pushed down the jewelled spear and stirred with it," and when the brine thickened and the spear was drawn up, "the brine that dripped down from the end of the spear was piled up and became an island. This is the Island of Onogoro." [6] Supported as the text's own content; this is the primary passage.

Read the grammar of that scene. The bridge is not a route between two finished realms. It is a work platform suspended over the not-yet-world, and the first act performed on it is not travel but stirring. Nothing crosses; something begins. The couple stand on the luminous arch the way a cook stands at a bowl, churn the sea until it curdles, lift the spear, and let the drops become the first island. Then the bridge does something no Norse structure ever does: it leaves the story. Once the land exists, the text has no further use for the span. The road's whole function was to be a place to stand while the world got made. It is scaffolding, and scaffolding is definitionally temporary.

Even the bridge's identity is unsettled at the root, and the oldest English translation says so honestly. Chamberlain's footnote: "The best authorities are at variance as to the nature of this bridge uniting Heaven with Earth." [6] The rainbow reading is a modern interpretive suggestion, not a statement of the text: the standard encyclopedia article on Ame-no-ukihashi says "The concept of the Floating Bridge may have been inspired by the rainbow," while noting it "has also been suggested that it represents the Milky Way." [7] Tri-state: the bridge itself is Supported as text; the rainbow identification is interpretation, Unproven as a claim about the original image; the Milky Way rival exists and is recorded. This lens uses the bridge for its function, not its color, and the function is clear either way.

What happens to the bridge afterward completes the arc. At Ninigi's descent to earth, the local god Sarutahiko stands at or near the bridge and blocks the way, and Ame-no-Uzume must charm him aside before the passage proceeds; the road acquires a guardian, as Bifröst has Heimdall, but the resemblance ends there. [7] The Tango Fudoki tradition says the bridge later collapsed outright, its remains becoming land west of Kyoto, the road subsiding into geography. [7] And the nineteenth-century nativist Hayashi Ōen held that the bridge still exists but held, in the source's summary of his view (paraphrase, not his words), that the bridge survives invisible and intangible except to the purified. [7] CORRECTION (grader pass): the earlier phrasing dressed the paraphrase as a quotation and dropped the nativist context of his reading. Follow that sequence and you have the whole internalizing gradient in miniature, three moves long: the road becomes the ground, then the road becomes an inward condition, visible only to the prepared. A span that began as a carpenter's platform for creation ends as a state of purity. Nothing in the Norse material makes that turn. Snorri's bridge stays a bridge, right up to the day it breaks under riders from Muspell.

One more turn of the knife, and it cuts toward the poster. The bridge in the Kojiki is used, once, by the least exalted of motions: churning. The register's Japanese debut is domestic, culinary, almost comic, two deities stirring the ocean like a pot, and the humor is not incidental. When the sun goddess later hides in the cave and the world goes dark, it is again Uzume, dancing on an overturned tub, whose striptease makes the gods "laugh heartily" and lures the light back out; the mirror hung at the cave mouth shows Amaterasu her own reflection and she steps toward it. [8] Light returns to the world in Japan by laughter and by a mirror, not by a road. Keep the mirror in view. The Huayan Buddhists will take it and multiply it past counting.

## III. China: the arch is a creature, and you read it, you do not ride it

The Chinese record makes the sharpest possible refusal of the bridge. The oracle bone script drew the rainbow, the character 虹, as what the standard etymological reference calls a pictograph of a two-headed arc-shaped being, and states plainly: "The ancient Chinese regarded the rainbow as a two-headed dragon." [9] One of the surviving divination inscriptions reads "有出虹自北飲于河," which the same entry renders "A rainbow came forth from the north and drank from the River." [9] Supported as inscription and gloss. Sit with the image: an arch that stoops to drink, mouths at both ends, present in the diviner's ledger the way a portent is present, as something to be interpreted, not traversed.

The Shang diviner who recorded that omen was doing the characteristic Chinese thing with the bow: filing it. A rainbow in the earliest records is not a route to another realm; it is an entry in a correspondence file between heaven and the king, a sign to be read, an event with a direction (from the north) and an appetite (for the Yellow River). The modern encyclopedia summary agrees with the glyph: "In Chinese mythology, Hong is a two-headed dragon that represents the rainbow," though the article carries no citation for the sentence, so take it as corroboration of the Wiktionary glyph account rather than an independent authority; the strong version of the claim rests on the oracle bones, which is the right place for it to rest. [10] The one love story in the register stays within the arch rather than crossing it: in a folktale recorded by Lee and Fraser, the lovers Hsienpo and Yingt'ai, the red and the blue of the double bow, "must wait until the rainbow appears to be alone together." [10] The rainbow is not their road. It is their private room.

Where the register does touch the sky's fabric, it builds repair, not passage. The Huainanzi, chapter 6 in the standard modern translation, tells of the catastrophe when heaven's pillars broke: "Thereupon, Nüwa smelted together five-colored stones in order to patch up the azure sky," before propping the firmament on a turtle's legs. [11] The five colors are mortar. They close a crack; they do not open a lane. Compare the Hefferlin manuscript's Western fantasy, "multicoloured buildings, streets and roads," a city paved in the bow, and the difference in instinct is total: given a broken sky, the Antarctic account dreams of building the rainbow as real estate, while the Han text smelts it into caulk. [5] One register pave, one register patch.

So in the Chinese material the arch is a being and a sign. It drinks. It gets filed. It is genders and lovers and an omen of things to come, and in every attestation this lens could verify it is something the rainbow does, not something you go down. Tri-state the whole section: the oracle-bone inscription and glyph are Supported; the two-headed dragon as the standard ancient construal is Supported through the etymological reference and echoed, less rigorously, by the encyclopedia; the Nüwa passage is Supported as the Huainanzi's content via the modern translation it cites. What is not found, anywhere in the verified material, is a Chinese text making the rainbow a road. That absence is itself the finding, and it is as close to a clean null result as cultural history offers.

The omen habit is worth one more beat, because it is the habit the poster unknowingly imitates. A diviner who records "a rainbow came forth from the north and drank from the River" is converting spectacle into interpretation, marking the sign, hedging the conclusion. The poster's closing move, "Coincidence, inspiration, or a hint that they knew? NO DOCUMENTED DIRECT LINK," is the same gesture in desktop-publishing clothing: the register raises a possibility and immediately files it under what the evidence supports. [2] The audit confirmed the label is honest. [12] The oldest rainbow readers in the record would recognize the form, even if they filed their omens on bone.

## IV. Huayan: the mirror made cosmological

The third station dissolves the road by the most radical means available: it deletes distance. The Buddhāvataṃsaka Sūtra, the Flower Ornament Scripture of third-century Mahayana Buddhism, and the Huayan school that formed around it in sixth- to eighth-century China, offer the image of Indra's net, Indrajāla: an infinite net hung over the palace of the god Indra on Mount Meru, with "a multifaceted jewel at each vertex, with each jewel being reflected in all of the other jewels." [13] Supported as the tradition's own doctrine; the following details all trace to the same reference.

The patriarch Dushun's treatise on the fivefold practice of Huayan gives the extended description: the net is "made entirely of jewels," and "within each jewel, simultaneously, is reflected the whole net," so that to sit in any one jewel is to sit in all of them. [13] Fazang, the third patriarch, demonstrated the doctrine for Empress Wu with a golden lion, and the tradition often retells the demonstration with a statue ringed by mirrors; the cited article records the lion and the Indra's-net comparison. [13] CORRECTION (grader pass, 2026-09-29): an earlier line imported the mirrors and a mirror count past the cited source, which carries the lion only. The doctrinal name for what the image teaches is perfect interfusion, yuánróng, 圓融: per the philosopher Bryan Van Norden as cited in the same article, each thing depends "for both its existence and its identity upon every other thing that exists." [13]

Now set the two cosmologies side by side, because they are answers to the same question. Snorri's question is: how do the gods get from Asgard to the other worlds? His answer is a single guarded cable, strong, three-colored, fire-banded against giants, scheduled to snap at Ragnarök. The Huayan question is: what is the relation of any one thing to any other? The answer is that there is no gap to bridge, because each jewel already contains the reflection of every other. Where the Norse road is a line between two points, Indra's net makes every point the whole. The bridge problem does not get solved. It gets revealed as a mistake. There is nowhere to travel because you were never anywhere else.

This is the internalization thesis carried to its cosmological limit. Notice what the imagery did on its way there. The Shinto bridge was a platform you stand on; the Chinese dragon was a creature you read; the Huayan net is a condition you are already in. The light is no longer even something you follow. It is the fact that every surface, including you, is doing at all times, whether or not anyone looks. Fazang needed a golden lion and an Empress to make a court see it. The poster's footer needs eleven words. "Follow the light within" is, read through Huayan eyes, a simplification in the right direction: the within was never separate from the without to begin with, since every within reflects every without, infinitely, in both directions.

There is also a colder observation available, and this lens is obliged to make it. Indra's net is the mirror thesis of the digest's own Q-operation vocabulary, "a surface on which receptions occur," taken cosmic. [2] Every jewel is a surface on which every other jewel occurs. A rainbow poster that hangs eight cultural receptions in one frame, Snorri, aurora, Antarctica, four maps, a racing game, is a small net of this kind: each panel reflects the others, and the poster insists, in its footer, that the reflections are the quest. The difference between the poster and the sutra is that the sutra knows the net is the point. The poster half-knows. Its panels still believe they are leading somewhere, downward through time, toward 1992. In the Huayan reading the panels are not a road. They are jewels on one string, and any of them already contains the show.

Tri-state: all textual claims in this section are Supported through the reference above. What is Unproven, and should stay marked, is any claim about historical transmission, that is, any suggestion that the poster or Mario Kart's designers consciously drew on Huayan imagery. The audit found no documented direct link for the Norse-to-Nintendo chain, and the same verdict applies a fortiori here. [12] The net is offered as a lens, not a lineage.

## V. Tibet: the road you become, then the road you stop being

The last station is the one where the internalization finishes, because there the bridge stops being a metaphor and becomes a body, and then stops being a body too. Dzogchen, the Great Perfection tradition of Tibetan Buddhism, holds as its fruit the rainbow body, Tibetan 'ja' lus, Wylie 'ja' lus, described in the standard reference as "a level of realization where the physical body transmutes into light." [14] The doctrine is specific about mechanism and residue. Through tögal, the direct visionary practice of Dzogchen, the practitioner works toward "a body of pure light and the dissolution of the physical body at death"; if the practice is incomplete, the dying body "starts to shrink until he or she" vanishes, and "Usually fingernails, toenails and hair are left behind." [14] The perfected variant, the rainbow body of great transference, 'ja' lus 'pho ba chen po, is attained by exceptional practitioners who finish the four visions before death and self-liberate "without leaving behind 'the hair or fingernails' or dying," into a body of light the tradition names sambhogakaya. [14] Supported as doctrine: this is what the texts teach, and the tradition links the idea to practices old in Tibet, with the same article noting pre-Buddhist Bon connections. [14]

The claimed instances must be tri-stated with unusual care, because here the register's evidence is witnesses, not instruments. The reference lists the cases the tradition names: Shardza Tashi Gyaltsen (1935), Sodnam Namgyal (1952), Ayu Khandro (1953), Togden Ugyen Tendzin (1962), and Khenpo A-chos (1998), the last cited to a 2002 account in the review of the Institute of Noetic Sciences. [14] Verdicts, separately and honestly: the existence of this case tradition is Supported, meaning it is documented that these reports exist and circulate within the tradition and its modern chroniclers. The physical events themselves, bodies shrinking or dissolving, are Unproven in the sense this run's audit language uses: no independent, instrumented verification is cited in any source consulted, and the reports are eyewitness testimony gathered and transmitted by devotees. A shrinkage claim made at altitude in 1935 Kham and passed orally before it was written is not nothing, but it is not a measurement.

The Khenpo A-chö case is the one this lens was told to mark, and it deserves the marking. The case entered modern circulation through Christian-Buddhist correspondence and then through a book: Father Francis V. Tiso's "Rainbow Body and Resurrection: Spiritual Attainment, the Dissolution of the Material Body, and the Case of Khenpo A Chö," published by North Atlantic Books in 2016. [15] The publisher's page says the book "chronicles the dissolution of Khenpo's material body within a week of his death, including eye-witness interviews," and gives the death as in eastern Tibet in 1999. [15] Note the discrepancy and keep it: most secondary accounts, including the encyclopedia entry above, give the year as 1998; the publisher's own page gives 1999. [14] [15] Tri-state: the case is Supported as a documented testimony corpus with named investigators; the exact year is Unproven (sources disagree); the event is Unproven as a physical occurrence. Tiso's own posture, notably, matches the honest-label shape: the same encyclopedia entry lists, in its further reading, a later methodological article by Tiso on how rainbow-body claims should be researched. [14] The register's chief Western archivist is also one of its auditors.

Tiso's book also discloses the comparison driving his interest: the question, per the publisher, "whether there is a connection between the rainbow body and the dying and rising of Jesus." [15] That comparison is a Western appetite looking eastward for a bridge of its own, resurrection to resurrection, and this lens notes the symmetry without endorsing it: the East internalized its road; the West now wants to internalize its miracle. Both parties are reaching for the same junction where road and traveler stop being two.

What does the Tibetan register actually claim, in the end? Not that there is a road to heaven. That heaven's light is the practitioner's own substance, and the journey's last phase is discovering the road was made of you. Set Snorri beside it once more. In Gylfaginning the red of the bow is burning fire on the structure, a defensive battlement that keeps the giants off; in Dzogchen the fire is in the walker, the luminosity is the body reaching completion, and the destination is not a place the road leads to but the state in which road, walker, and light stop differing. The Norse bridge breaks, once, at the end of the world, under enemy cavalry. The rainbow body dissolves, quietly, at the end of a life, under no one's weight at all. One structure fails from outside; the other finishes from within. That is the whole thesis in two images.

## VI. The register, measured through the Eastern lens

**IS.** Through the Eastern lens the rainbow register is a grammar of self-location, not a system of transport. Its four Eastern stations are four answers to the question "where is the luminous path, really": under your feet at the beginning of things (the floating bridge, a platform used once, for stirring), in the sky as a creature to be read (the drinking dragon of the oracle bones), in the structure of everything as mutual reflection (Indra's net, distance abolished), and in the body as its final substance (the rainbow body, dissolution as graduation). The Norse register, by contrast, is infrastructure: built, guarded, scheduled, ridden. Both registers use the same chromatic sign. The East uses it to name what the self is or could become. The West uses it to name where else one might go. The poster belongs, in every panel, to the second habit, and then, in its last line, says the first.

**DOES.** It converts distance into transformation. Every Eastern station performs the same operation on Snorri's problem: the problem of getting between realms is replaced by the problem of becoming what would not need to get there. The Kojiki's couple do not cross the bridge to a ready world; they stir the world into existence from the bridge. The Shang diviner does not ride the dragon; he reads it and adjusts his conduct. The Huayan master does not travel to the other jewels; he shows the court that travel was always the wrong verb. The Dzogchen practitioner does not ascend at death; the ascending is what dissolution means. The register also converts spectacle into discipline: in every station the light is paired with preparation, purification, practice, a curriculum. Rainbow body is the fruit of tögal, not of watching the sky. The poster performs this conversion once, accidentally, at its bottom edge.

**WANTS.** It wants the reader to stop looking up. That sentence is the operator's framing and this lens endorses it with evidence: the Eastern stations progress, in sequence, from an external bridge (used, then discarded, then collapsed, then made invisible), through a sign in the sky (to be interpreted, never ridden), to conditions in which "up" has stopped being the direction of the sacred at all. Indra's net has no up. The rainbow body has no up. The register's want, at full expression, is the closure of the vertical axis: the ladder the poster's Higher Realms still implies is, in the Eastern materials, a ladder that gets folded into the climber. And this is exactly what the poster's own footer preaches. "FOLLOW THE LIGHT WITHIN" is Eastern doctrine arriving as Western poster art; the panels still look up and across, at stars, at maps, at an Antarctic horizon, at a racetrack in the clouds, while the small type at the bottom tells the truth about where the tradition finally located the road. Whether the designer intended the convergence is Unproven; the audit standard applies, no documented link in either direction. [12] What is measurable is that the poster's two halves disagree about direction, and that the Eastern materials side with the bottom half.

**LEAVES.** Inventory the residue, because a register is known by its leavings. The Norse bridge leaves ruins in narrative: a snapped span at the eschaton. The Eastern stations each leave a different remainder. Shinto leaves an island, Onogoro, and then a legend that the bridge itself collapsed into real estate west of Kyoto. [6] [7] China leaves glyphs and divination records, an alphabet in which the rainbow still wears its two heads. [9] Huayan leaves mirrors, the golden lion of Fazang, and a doctrine in which nothing is left out because nothing was ever separate. [13] Tibet leaves hair and fingernails, per the doctrine's own accounting, and a corpus of witnesses. [14] That last remainder is the register's signature liability: at the moment of its most extreme claim, the Eastern register substitutes testimony for instrumentation, and every honest treatment, Tiso's included, has to tri-state the shrinkages as reports. [14] [15] The poster, interestingly, leaves the same kind of residue on purpose: its honest labels, LATER ACCOUNT, UNVERIFIED, NO DOCUMENTED DIRECT LINK, are the audit's own verdicts pre-emptively applied, the register filing its own omens. [2] [12]

**GOING. SPECULATION, as instructed.** Where the register goes next, through this lens, is further inward and simultaneously, in the market, further outward, and the two movements are not in conflict. The interior forms keep their corridors: Dzogchen practice lineages are alive, and the rainbow body vocabulary now circulates in Western contemplative and wellness idiom as "light body" talk, a domestication this lens notes without endorsing; the claim that specific modern teachers dissolving into light continues to rest on the same witness-only evidence that made it Unproven above. The exterior forms keep their franchises: Super Mario Kart shipped in 1992 and Rainbow Road, the final track of the Special Cup, suspended over a starfield, became the register's widest distribution event, with the audit supporting the date, the track, and its recurrence in every later entry, while declining, correctly, to attach a number to "global phenomenon." [12] Speculative reading: mass culture has re-externalized the road, turned the inward gradient back into a surface you cross at speed, walled in void, and put a million hands on a wheel. Players in the tens of millions, on audited unit counts, have crossed a rainbow road in the Norse direction, as a route with guardrails and falling, which is to say the register now runs in both directions at once, outer form for the crowd, inner form for the practitioners, and the poster, which accidentally carries both, is a fair emblem of the split. The speculation stops there.

## VII. Close: the line the poster dropped

The audit's most quietly important finding is an omission. The poster quotes Snorri on the bridge's strength and three colors, and drops the same chapter's next beat: "That which thou seest to be red in the bow is burning fire." [3] The audit flags the omission because the poster elsewhere leans on fire and light imagery; the source itself already contained the fire. [3] This lens flags it for a different reason. The dropped line is the hinge between the two halves of the poster. Fire on the bridge, keeping giants off, is the Norse answer. Fire as the substance of the walker, the body completing itself as light, is the Eastern answer, and it is the answer the footer preaches. The poster cut exactly the sentence that would have shown it which one it was quoting.

Same Earth, Higher Realms, Many Perspectives, One Human Quest: the poster's own footer line, tested against this lens, holds. The perspectives are real and they do not say the same thing. In the North the bow is a bridge, and you may cross it if you are a god, and it will break. In Japan it is a platform for making worlds, used once, then folded into purity. In China it is a two-headed dragon with an appetite for rivers, filed by diviners as an omen. In the Huayan cosmos it is a net in which every jewel is already every other, travel exposed as a category error. In Tibet it is what a finished human being turns into, on the testimony of witnesses and on no instrument's word. The quest is one because the question is one: where is the between, and who crosses it. The Eastern answer, station by station, is that the between narrows with every retelling until the traveler and the road are the same width. The road you become is a road you cannot fall from. Follow the light within: the poster wrote Eastern doctrine in its margin and then spent eight panels looking up. The East would tell it gently that it had the answer the whole time, and that the answer was never at the end of the bridge.
